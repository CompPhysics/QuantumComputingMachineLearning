Chapter 1: Introduction and Motivation#

This chapter sets the stage for the book by discussing the motivation for using quantum computing in many-body physics. We explain the exponential growth of Hilbert space in interacting many-body systems and why classical simulation methods become infeasible as system size increases. The historical context of quantum simulation is presented, from Feynman’s original idea to modern experiments with near-term quantum devices. We also highlight recent initiatives (such as TALENT quantum computing courses and new quantum hardware) that have renewed interest in this field. The chapter concludes with an overview of the book’s structure and key goals.

The problem#

Almost everything interesting in physics involves many interacting particles. The electrons of a molecule, the nucleons of a nucleus, the spins of a magnet, the quarks and gluons of a hadron, the atoms in an optical lattice: in every case the quantities one wants, a binding energy, a spectrum, a response function, a phase diagram, follow in principle from a Hamiltonian that can be written on one line and a Schr”odinger equation whose solution is, in practice, out of reach. The reason is not that the equation is complicated. It is that its solution is large. A system of \(N\) fermions distributed over \(M\) single-particle states has a Hilbert space of dimension \(\binom{M}{N}\), and a chain of \(n\) spins one of dimension \(2^n\); doubling the system squares the dimension, and no reorganization of the calculation removes that. Chapter 3 gives the numbers. Forty single-particle states at half filling is a space of \(10^{11}\) dimensions, the limit of what an iterative eigensolver on a large parallel machine can handle; eighty states is \(10^{23}\), more than the number of bytes of storage on Earth; a \(6\times6\) Hubbard cluster at half filling has \(8\times10^{19}\) states, and \(^{16}\)O in a modest model space of forty orbitals per nucleon species has \(6\times10^{15}\) determinants. This is the exponential wall, and it stands between us and the exact solution of every problem in this book.

Many-body theory is the art of not solving the Schr”odinger equation exactly. Mean-field theory replaces the interacting problem by a product state in a self-consistent potential and captures, at polynomial cost, the bulk of the energy and none of the correlations; perturbation theory and coupled-cluster theory build the correlations back in with a polynomial number of parameters, and are exact in a limit that is rarely reached; configuration interaction truncates the space and converges slowly; quantum Monte Carlo samples the space stochastically and, for fermions, pays for it with a sign problem that grows exponentially in the particle number or the inverse temperature; tensor networks compress the state and succeed exactly when the entanglement is small, which in one dimension is often and in more is not. Each of these has produced results of lasting value, and each has a regime in which it fails, and the regimes of failure overlap: strongly correlated fermions in two or three dimensions, real-time dynamics far from equilibrium, and the spectra of systems with many competing scales are hard for all of them at once. The exponential wall is not an inconvenience of the current methods but a property of the problem, and the question this book asks is what a computer whose own state space grows exponentially can do about it.

Feynman’s idea and what became of it#

The idea is older than the machines. In 1981, at a conference on the physics of computation, Richard Feynman asked whether a classical computer could simulate quantum mechanics efficiently, answered that in general it could not, since the number of amplitudes to be tracked grows exponentially with the size of the system, and proposed the obvious remedy: build a computer out of quantum-mechanical elements, whose state space grows in the same way, and let one quantum system imitate another [Feynman1982]. The proposal was a thought experiment for a decade. Deutsch formalized the notion of a universal quantum computer, Shor’s factoring algorithm of 1994 showed that such a machine could do something classical computers apparently cannot, and in 1996 Lloyd showed that Feynman’s simulator was universal in the sense that matters here: any local Hamiltonian can be simulated by a sequence of short evolutions under its pieces, the Trotter formula of Chapter 3, with a cost that grows polynomially in the size of the system and the length of the evolution [Lloyd1996]. Abrams and Lloyd then showed how to turn simulated dynamics into eigenvalues, by phase estimation [AbramsLloyd1999], and Aspuru-Guzik and collaborators worked out in 2005 what it would cost to compute the ground-state energy of a molecule that way [AspuruGuzik2005]. By then the algorithmic side of quantum simulation was, in outline, complete: map the fermions to qubits, prepare a trial state, evolve it under the Hamiltonian, read off the energy with a phase register. Chapters 4 to 6 of this book follow that outline.

The machines caught up slowly and then quickly. The first demonstrations, on two or three qubits of nuclear spins, trapped ions or superconducting circuits, computed the energy of the hydrogen molecule or simulated a two-site model, and their point was that the outline could be executed at all. Between 2014 and 2020 a second family of algorithms appeared, adapted to machines with tens of noisy qubits and no error correction: the variational quantum eigensolver of Peruzzo and collaborators [Peruzzo2014], which replaces the deep coherent circuit of phase estimation by a shallow parametrized one and a classical optimizer, and its relatives, which are the subject of Chapter 7. Preskill named the era, noisy intermediate-scale quantum computing [Preskill2018], and predicted with some accuracy what it would and would not deliver. For nuclear physics the entry point was the calculation of the deuteron binding energy on a cloud-accessible superconducting processor in 2018 [Dumitrescu2018], a two- and three-qubit calculation whose importance was not the number but the demonstration that the machinery of effective field theory, the Jordan–Wigner mapping and a variational circuit could be put together end to end. Since then the field has computed the spectra of small nuclei and lattice gauge theories, the dynamics of spin models on a hundred or more qubits at depths where classical simulation becomes laborious [Kim2023], and, most consequentially, has crossed the threshold at which a logical qubit encoded in many physical ones is better than its constituents [Acharya2025,Bluvstein2024]. That last result is the one that makes the fault-tolerant algorithms of Chapters 6 and 8 a matter of engineering rather than of principle, and it is the reason this book treats them on an equal footing with the near-term ones.

The community around these questions has organized itself accordingly. The long-range planning exercises of nuclear physics in the United States and Europe now list quantum information science among their priorities [Beck2023]; the TALENT schools (Training in Advanced Low Energy Nuclear Theory), from which parts of this book descend, have in recent years run courses on quantum computing and quantum machine learning for nuclear physics; and university curricula that a decade ago offered quantum computing as an elective in the physics of information now offer it as a tool of many-body theory. This book is written for the graduate student or researcher who arrives from the many-body side, knows what a Slater determinant and a coupled-cluster amplitude are, and wants to know, concretely and with code, what a quantum computer does with them.

What a quantum computer is, and is not, for a many-body physicist#

It is worth being precise at the outset about the kind of advantage that is on offer, because the subject attracts both overstatement and its reaction. A register of \(n\) qubits holds a state of \(2^n\) complex amplitudes, and a circuit of polynomial depth transforms all of them at once. That is the source of every advantage in this book, and it is a source with three restrictions attached. First, the amplitudes cannot be read: a measurement returns \(n\) bits drawn from the distribution of the squared amplitudes and destroys the state, so that every quantity one wants must be arranged to appear as the statistics of a few bits over many repetitions, and Chapters 4 and 7 show what that costs. Second, the state must be prepared: an eigenstate of an interacting Hamiltonian is no more available on a quantum computer than on a classical one, and the algorithms that extract its energy exactly, phase estimation and its descendants, need a trial state whose overlap with it is not exponentially small. That overlap is the quantity on which the promise of exponential advantage for ground-state problems hinges, and it is not known to be large in general [Lee2023]. Third, the machines are noisy, and the depth of a circuit that can be executed before the noise overwhelms the signal is, on current hardware, of the order of a few hundred to a few thousand two-qubit gates, a budget that the algorithms of Chapter 6 exceed by orders of magnitude and the algorithms of Chapter 7 were designed to respect. Quantum error correction, Chapter 9, removes the third restriction at a large constant cost in qubits, and does nothing about the first two.

What survives these restrictions is real. Real-time dynamics is the clearest case: a classical computer must store the state, and a quantum computer need only propagate it, so that the evolution of an interacting system for a time \(t\) costs resources polynomial in \(t\) and in the size of the system, which no classical method achieves in general; this is Lloyd’s theorem, and it is the content of Chapter 5 and the reason the lattice gauge theories of Chapter 10, whose real-time and finite-density physics is closed to lattice Monte Carlo by the sign problem, are the application with the strongest motivation. Eigenvalues are the second case, with the overlap caveat above: given a state that is a fair approximation to an eigenstate, phase estimation refines its energy to a precision exponential in the number of qubits spent on it, a guarantee no variational method has. And the variational algorithms, whose guarantees are weaker, are the ones that run today and the ones through which most of what is known empirically about these machines has been learned. The honest summary is that quantum computers move the difficulty of the many-body problem rather than remove it, from the size of the state to its preparation and measurement, and the book is organized around seeing exactly where it goes.

The approach of this book#

Three decisions shape the book. The first is to follow a small number of model Hamiltonians all the way through. Chapter 3 introduces four of them, the Lipkin model, the pairing model, the Hubbard model and the Heisenberg model, each a caricature of a class of physical systems, each exactly solvable at small size by symmetry, and each the standard test case for some family of methods. They are then solved classically by exact diagonalization, Hartree–Fock and coupled cluster in Chapter 3, mapped to qubits in Chapter 4, time-evolved in Chapter 5, handed to phase estimation in Chapter 6 and to the variational algorithms in Chapter 7, and filtered in Chapter 8, at the same sizes and couplings throughout, so that the tables of different chapters can be laid side by side. The second decision is that every number in the book is produced by a program the reader can run. The programs are written in Python on numpy and matplotlib alone, and they are not calls to a quantum-computing framework: the state-vector simulator, the gates, the circuits, the Pauli sums, the Jordan–Wigner mapping, the Trotter steps, the phase-estimation register and the variational loop are all written out, in a few hundred lines each, so that nothing is hidden and everything can be modified. Each chapter ends with a list of its programs and is accompanied by a Jupyter notebook, generated from the chapter text, that executes every listing and every program in order. The frameworks that run on real hardware, Qiskit, Cirq, PennyLane and their relatives, are introduced in Appendix A once the reader knows what they do, and the translation from the book’s simulator to any of them is mechanical. A reader who works through the notebooks will have, at the end, a complete and understood toolkit; a reader who only reads will still find every claim backed by a table whose provenance is stated.

The third decision is a matter of tone. Quantum computing for many-body physics is a young field in which the gap between what has been proposed and what has been demonstrated is wide, and the book tries to keep the two apart. Where an algorithm has a theorem behind it, the theorem is stated and, when it is short, proved; where a method is heuristic, the book says so and shows, on the models, both where it works and where it fails, since the failures of variational circuits and of trial states are as instructive as their successes and considerably less advertised. Where the answer to a question is not known, whether the overlap problem is benign for nuclei, whether variational circuits can be trained at scale, whether any of this beats the best classical methods on a problem anyone cares about, the book says that too.

A guide to the chapters#

The book has three parts, though they are not marked as such. Chapters 2 to 4 are foundations. Chapter 2 sets out the gate model of quantum computation in the language of linear algebra that a physicist already speaks: qubits, the Bloch sphere, Pauli matrices, tensor products, measurement, density matrices and decoherence, entanglement, the elementary gates and the circuits built from them, with superdense coding and teleportation as first examples of what entanglement buys and a variational calculation on a two-level system as a first glimpse of Chapter 7. Chapter 3 is the many-body side of the same foundations: second quantization, the four models, and the three classical methods, exact diagonalization, Hartree–Fock and coupled cluster, against which every quantum result in the book is measured, ending with the unitary coupled-cluster ansatz whose evaluation is exactly the thing a classical computer cannot do and a quantum computer can. Chapter 4 joins the two: the Jordan–Wigner transformation turns the second-quantized Hamiltonians into sums of Pauli strings, symmetries reduce the number of qubits and strings, and three circuits follow, the exponential of a Pauli string, the Trotter step and the measurement of an energy, which are the building blocks of everything after.

Chapters 5 to 8 are algorithms. Chapter 5 is Hamiltonian simulation: the Trotter–Suzuki decomposition of \(e^{-i\hat{H}t}\) and its error, the higher-order formulas, the modern alternatives of qubitization and quantum signal processing, real-time dynamics of the lattice models, and the non-unitary evolutions, imaginary time in particular, that can be embedded in a unitary at the price of a probability of success. Chapter 6 is the quantum Fourier transform and phase estimation, the algorithm that turns simulated dynamics into eigenvalues, applied to the four models with a careful account of the two errors, the finite register and the Trotterized evolution, and of the trial state; the Harrow–Hassidim–Lloyd algorithm for linear systems is presented there as an application of the same register to the resolvent of a many-body Hamiltonian. Chapter 7 is the variational family: the variational quantum eigensolver, its ansätze, its measurement cost, its gradients and its failure modes, applied to the same four models and compared with phase estimation; the adaptive version that builds its own circuit; and the quantum approximate optimization algorithm, with a glance at quantum machine learning. Chapter 8 is the rodeo algorithm and its relatives, which use controlled evolutions and measurements to filter an eigenstate out of a trial state, and which sit between phase estimation and the variational methods in what they require and what they guarantee. Because every filter needs a trial state with a large overlap, the chapter begins with resolution refinement, which carries an eigenstate adiabatically from a coarse model space to a fine one; it runs the combination on all four models, and it ends with a gate-level depth budget that collects the bottlenecks met along the way.

Chapters 9 to 11 are about the machines and the frontier. Chapter 9 is quantum error correction: the error models, the repetition and Shor codes, syndrome measurement, the surface code and the threshold theorem, and the error-mitigation techniques that stand in for error correction on present hardware. Chapter 10 is a case study in the direction the field is heading, the simulation of a lattice gauge theory, the Schwinger model in one spatial dimension, from the Kogut–Susskind Hamiltonian through the mapping of matter and gauge fields to qubits to circuits for its dynamics and observables, with results from exact diagonalization and from quantum hardware and a discussion of what changes in more dimensions and with non-Abelian gauge groups. Chapter 11 closes the book with an assessment of where the hardware stands, of what the analog simulators, the annealers and the hybrid methods add to the gate-model picture, and of the open problems. Appendix A collects the software tools, and Appendix B the mathematical background. Table 1.2 gives the map in one place.

How to read this book#

The prerequisites are a first course in quantum mechanics, linear algebra at the level of eigenvalue problems and tensor products, and some acquaintance with second quantization, all of which Chapters 2 and 3 and Appendix B review at the pace of a reminder rather than a first encounter. No prior knowledge of quantum computing is assumed. A reader who has it can begin at Chapter 3; a reader who comes from quantum information rather than many-body physics should read Chapter 3 with care, since the models and the classical methods it introduces are what every later chapter computes and compares against. The chapters are meant to be read in order through Chapter 7, after which Chapters 8, 9 and 10 can be taken in any order.

A few conventions hold throughout and are worth fixing now, because the literature does not agree on them. Qubit \(0\) is the leftmost factor of a tensor product and the most significant bit of a computational basis state, so that \(\vert q_0q_1\cdots q_{n-1}\rangle\) is read as a binary number from the left; in the occupation-number encodings, \(\vert 1\rangle\) is an occupied mode. Circuits are read from left to right, operators from right to left. Rotations are \(R_a(\theta)=e^{-i\theta\sigma_a/2}\), so that a full turn is \(\theta=2\pi\) and changes the sign of the state. Hamiltonians carry a hat, \(\hat{H}\), and their qubit images are written as Pauli sums \(\sum_\alpha h_\alpha P_\alpha\) with strings \(P_\alpha\) of the matrices \(I\), \(X\), \(Y\), \(Z\). Energies are quoted in the units of the model, with \(\hbar=1\) throughout. The exercises at the end of each chapter range from short derivations to numerical projects that extend the chapter’s programs, and the programs, which are listed at the end of each chapter and collected in the accompanying repository, are as much a part of the book as the text.
