Chapter 11: Conclusion and Outlook#

In the final chapter we summarize the key concepts covered in the book and reflect on future directions. We discuss the current status of quantum hardware (superconducting qubits, trapped ions, Rydberg atoms) and their applicability to many-body physics. Emerging topics such as analog quantum simulators, adiabatic quantum computing, and hybrid quantum-classical models are briefly mentioned. We outline open challenges (scaling to larger systems, improving algorithms, mitigating noise) and encourage further research. The chapter concludes by outlining future research directions in quantum simulation and many-body theory.

What the book has shown#

The book began with a wall and ends with a map of the ways around it. The wall, the exponential growth of the many-body Hilbert space, was stated in Chapter 1 and quantified in Chapter 3, where the four models that run through the book, Lipkin, pairing, Hubbard and Heisenberg, were solved exactly at small size and approximately by Hartree–Fock and coupled cluster, and where the unitary coupled-cluster ansatz appeared as the first object whose evaluation is natural on a quantum computer and unnatural on a classical one. Chapter 4 turned the second-quantized Hamiltonians into sums of Pauli strings and showed that the cost of a model on a quantum computer is measured by three numbers, qubits, strings and weight, each of which a symmetry can reduce; the Lipkin, pairing and Heisenberg models all became four-qubit problems of two-qubit strings, and the eight-qubit Hubbard ring kept the parity strings that are the price of fermions. From the Pauli sum three circuits followed mechanically, the Pauli rotation, the Trotter step and the measurement of an energy, and everything in the later chapters was built from them.

The algorithms then divided along a line that the book has drawn many times. On one side stand the methods that need a deep coherent circuit and give a guarantee: Hamiltonian simulation in Chapter 5, whose Trotter error is controlled and whose cost is polynomial in the system size and the evolution time; phase estimation in Chapter 6, which converts that simulated evolution into an eigenvalue with a precision that doubles with every control qubit, and whose application to the four models showed the accuracy governed entirely by the register and the time step and not by the difficulty of the system; and the rodeo algorithm and its relatives in Chapter 8, which use the same controlled evolutions to project an eigenstate out of a trial state with one reusable ancilla, an error that falls exponentially with the number of cycles, and a probabilistic, heralded outcome whose success rate is the overlap of the trial state, which resolution refinement was introduced to make large; compiled to gates, the pairing calculation of that chapter came to \(10^4\) to \(10^5\) two-qubit gates on five to nine qubits. On the other side stand the variational methods of Chapter 7, which need only shallow circuits and many repetitions, give an upper bound and nothing more, and whose quality was seen to be a property of the ansatz rather than of the algorithm: a hardware-efficient circuit converged smoothly to the wrong particle-number sector of the pairing and Hubbard Hamiltonians, while circuits built from the physics of each model were exact with a handful of parameters, and the adaptive version, given a pool complete under the symmetries of the problem, found those circuits by itself. The two sides need each other, since the variational state with its overlap of \(0.9998\) is what makes a phase-estimation run succeed on the first attempt, and the phase-estimation run is what certifies the variational state; that combination is the standard plan for the fault-tolerant era, and the small calculations of this book are its rehearsal.

Chapter 9 explained why the fault-tolerant era is needed at all and how it will be reached: the error models, the codes that detect and correct them by measuring syndromes rather than states, the surface code whose threshold has now been crossed on hardware, and the mitigation techniques that, in the meantime, trade repetitions for accuracy on the variational side. Chapter 10 took the whole apparatus to the problem that motivates much of the effort in nuclear and particle physics, a lattice gauge theory whose real-time and finite-density physics is closed to classical Monte Carlo by the sign problem, and showed on the Schwinger model that the mapping, the circuits and the measurements of the earlier chapters extend to gauge fields, and at what cost. What the book has not shown is a single result beyond the reach of a classical computer. Every table in it was produced on a simulator of at most eight qubits, checked against exact diagonalization, and chosen to be checkable; that was the point, and it is also the honest state of the field for the problems of many-body physics, where the calculations that have been run on hardware are, with a few exceptions in dynamics, still ones a laptop can verify.

Where the difficulty went#

A theme has run through the book and deserves to be stated once more as the conclusion it is. Quantum computers do not remove the difficulty of the many-body problem; they move it, and the different algorithms move it to different places. In phase estimation the difficulty lives in state preparation, since the algorithm returns the energy of whatever eigenstate the trial state overlaps with, and in circuit depth, since every bit of precision doubles the number of applications of the evolution operator. In the variational methods it lives in the design of the ansatz, which decides whether the answer is close, and in the measurement count, which grows as the square of the inverse accuracy and of the Pauli norm of the Hamiltonian, and which no proposal so far reduces below that scaling. In Hamiltonian simulation it lives in the Trotter error, or in the ancilla overhead of the methods that replace Trotterization, and in the extraction of observables from the evolved state. In every case there is a quantity, the overlap, the measurement budget, the depth, that is polynomial for the small systems of this book and whose behaviour at large size is either unknown or known to be unfavourable in the worst case. The question of whether quantum computers offer an exponential advantage for ground-state problems reduces, as Chapter 6 discussed, to whether the overlap of an efficiently preparable state with the ground state decays polynomially or exponentially with the system size for the systems of interest, and the evidence, reviewed critically in [Lee2023], is that for the molecules and materials studied so far the answer is not the one the field had hoped for. For nuclei, whose ground states are often well approximated by a small number of configurations in a suitably chosen basis, and for real-time dynamics, where the advantage rests on Lloyd’s theorem and not on an overlap, the prospects are better, and it is no accident that the case study of Chapter 10 is a problem in dynamics.

The hardware#

Every algorithm in the book was run on a simulator, and it is worth closing with a sober account of the machines it was written for. Four platforms carry most of the effort, and they differ in the resources that matter for many-body simulation, the number of qubits, the fidelity and speed of the two-qubit gates, the connectivity of the qubits and the coherence time, in ways that make each the natural home of a different class of problem [Preskill2018,Daley2022]. Superconducting circuits, the platform of the largest commercial efforts, have hundreds of qubits on a chip with nearest-neighbour connectivity on a planar lattice, gate times of tens of nanoseconds, two-qubit gate errors that have fallen to a few parts in a thousand, and coherence times of a few hundred microseconds; the utility-scale dynamics experiments and the below-threshold surface-code experiments cited in Chapter 1 were performed on them, and their planar connectivity is a direct match to the surface code and to lattice models in two dimensions, while the parity strings of a fermionic mapping must be routed through swaps. Trapped ions have fewer qubits per trap, tens rather than hundreds, but all-to-all connectivity within a trap, the highest two-qubit gate fidelities demonstrated on any platform, and coherence times of seconds; their gates are slower by orders of magnitude, and their natural strength is in problems with long-range interactions and in the deep circuits with few qubits that phase estimation on small systems requires. Neutral atoms in optical tweezers combine hundreds to thousands of qubits, reconfigurable connectivity by moving the atoms, and, through the Rydberg blockade, entangling gates that have improved rapidly; the first logical-qubit experiments at scale were performed on them [Bluvstein2024], and the same platform, operated in an analog rather than a digital mode, is a quantum simulator of spin models in its own right. Photonic and spin-qubit platforms are less mature for the problems of this book and are not discussed here.

Two remarks apply to all of them. The first is that the numbers that matter for a many-body calculation are the product of the two-qubit gate error and the number of two-qubit gates in the circuit. A Trotter step of the eight-qubit Hubbard ring of Chapter 4 costs a few dozen CNOT gates, a phase-estimation run with ten control qubits costs thousands of Trotter steps, and a gate error of \(10^{-3}\) gives an error budget of a few hundred gates before the output is noise; the arithmetic is the reason the variational methods of Chapter 7 exist and the reason error correction is not optional. The second is that the demonstrations of error correction below threshold, on superconducting and on neutral-atom platforms, change the character of the projection. A logical qubit whose error falls as the code distance grows means that the depth budget is no longer fixed by the physics of the qubit but by the number of physical qubits one is willing to spend per logical one, of the order of a thousand for the surface code at useful error rates; the resource estimates for chemically relevant molecules and for nuclei in realistic model spaces, which run to millions of physical qubits and days of runtime, are the targets of the next decade of engineering, and they are being revised downwards by better algorithms as fast as the hardware improves.

Beyond the gate model#

This book has been about the gate model, in which a many-body problem is compiled into a sequence of discrete unitaries on qubits, because the gate model is universal, because its errors can be corrected, and because its algorithms have guarantees that can be stated as theorems. It is not the only way to use a quantum system to study another. The oldest is the analog quantum simulator, closest to Feynman’s original proposal, in which a controllable system, ultracold atoms in an optical lattice, trapped ions with tunable spin-spin couplings, Rydberg atoms in tweezer arrays, superconducting circuits operated as coupled oscillators, is engineered to have the Hamiltonian one wants and then simply observed [Bloch2012,Georgescu2014]. The Hubbard model of Chapter 3 has been realized this way with fermionic atoms at temperatures now approaching the scale of the antiferromagnetic exchange, the Heisenberg and transverse-field Ising models with ions and Rydberg atoms at hundreds of spins, and lattice gauge theories of the kind studied in Chapter 10 in the first small instances. Analog simulators have no error correction and no guarantee of accuracy, and their observables are limited to what the apparatus can measure; what they have is size and speed, and for the equilibrium and dynamical properties of lattice models in two dimensions they are ahead of any gate-model calculation and likely to remain so for some time. The relation between the two approaches is the relation between an experiment and a computation, and the field needs both.

Adiabatic quantum computation and quantum annealing occupy the ground between. The adiabatic theorem that motivated the QAOA ansatz of Chapter 7 can be run directly: prepare the ground state of a simple Hamiltonian, deform it slowly into the one of interest, and read out the result, with a running time set by the inverse square of the minimal gap along the path. As a model of computation it is equivalent to the gate model; as a practice, on the special-purpose annealers that implement transverse-field Ising Hamiltonians on thousands of qubits with limited coherence, it is a heuristic optimizer whose advantage over classical methods on any problem remains unestablished, and whose relevance to many-body physics lies mainly in its use as a sampler of classical spin-glass and, with some care, quantum spin-model configurations. The hybrid methods are the most recent and, for the near term, the most promising. Every variational algorithm of Chapter 7 is already hybrid, a quantum circuit inside a classical optimizer, and the pattern generalizes: a quantum computer can serve as the impurity solver inside a dynamical mean-field or embedding calculation, as the evaluator of a few hard matrix elements inside a classical configuration-interaction or coupled-cluster calculation, as the generator of samples for a classical tensor-network or neural-network state, or as the provider of a trial state that a classical method refines. In each of these the quantum resource is spent on the part of the problem where classical methods fail and the classical resource on everything else, which is where the exponential lives and where it does not, respectively. The quantum-machine-learning models glanced at in Chapter 7 belong here too, as parametrized circuits trained on data, and the question of whether they offer anything that a classical model does not is open in the same way, and for the same reasons, as the question for variational eigensolvers.

Open problems#

The problems that remain are of three kinds, and it is useful to keep them apart, since they call for different people. The first kind is hardware: more qubits, better gates, longer coherence, faster readout, and the engineering of error correction from a demonstration into a platform. This is not a problem many-body theorists will solve, but it is one they should watch, because the algorithms that will run first on error-corrected machines are those with the smallest constant factors, and those factors are set by choices, of encoding, of Trotter ordering, of symmetry sector, that are theirs to make. The second kind is algorithmic, and here the many-body theorist is the expert. State preparation is the largest of them: the overlap problem of Chapter 6 is a question about the structure of physical ground states, about which many-body theory knows a great deal and quantum information theory little, and the design of trial states with provably large overlap, from mean-field theory, from coupled cluster, from tensor networks, from adiabatic paths, is where the two fields most need each other. Measurement is the second: the \(\epsilon^{-2}\) scaling of the variational methods is a statement about sample means, but the prefactor, the Pauli norm of the Hamiltonian, depends on the basis and the mapping and has been reduced by orders of magnitude for molecules by better choices, and the corresponding work for nuclear and lattice Hamiltonians is in its infancy. The third is the ansatz, whether hand-designed, adaptive or learned, and the barren-plateau obstruction that limits all of them: the escape routes, symmetry-preserving circuits, physically motivated initializations and problem-specific pools, are exactly the places where physical insight enters, and where the failures documented in Chapter 7 turn into a research programme. Beyond these, the extraction of observables other than the energy, response functions, transition rates, scattering amplitudes and the real-time correlators of Chapter 10, is where the advantage of Hamiltonian simulation should first become visible, and where the methods, including the resolvent techniques of Chapter 6, are least developed.

The third kind of problem is one of judgement. Classical many-body methods have not stood still: tensor networks in two dimensions, neural-network quantum states, and the steady improvement of coupled-cluster, Monte Carlo and configuration-interaction methods have moved the boundary of what a classical computer can do every year, and every claim of quantum advantage is a claim about that moving boundary. The discipline the field needs is the one this book has tried to practise: to compare against the best classical method on the same problem, to state the error and its source, to distinguish a theorem from a heuristic and a demonstration from a result, and to say when something is not known. The problems of many-body physics are hard enough that no tool will be wasted, and the reader who has reached this page has the two toolkits, the classical one of Chapter 3 and the quantum one of the chapters that followed, in the same hands. That is, in the end, what the book was for. The interesting work is in using both.
