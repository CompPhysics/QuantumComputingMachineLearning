Chapter 7: Summary and Perspectives#

The closing chapter looks back over the models of the book with the reassessment of Chapter 1 in mind. It summarizes what the exact computations of the preceding chapters showed about each model, states what quantum machine learning has been shown to gain on classical and on quantum data, and sets out, as a priority list rather than a consensus, the directions we find most promising for the next years: learning quantum states, processes and dynamics; quantum measurements followed by classical learning; structured variational models; quantum reservoirs and fixed feature maps; and fault-tolerant learning primitives. It ends with the perspectives for algorithms, physical applications and hardware that the companion volume and this book share.

What the book has shown#

The four model chapters have built, from the one hybrid model of Eq. (1.1), a kernel method, an optimization algorithm, a neural network with a physics-informed variant, and a generative model, each with its classical counterpart beside it and each tested exactly on problems small enough to allow it. It is worth collecting what those exact computations showed, model by model, because the pattern is the evidence on which the rest of this chapter rests.

The quantum support-vector machine. A new kernel, not a new algorithm. The angle-encoding kernel is a product of cosines and is evaluated classically in \(\bigO(p)\) operations; the entangling feature map of Havlíček and coworkers is not known to be, and on the data set built from its own structure it is exact where the Gaussian kernel is not (Chapter 2). On the data sets one actually meets (moons, circles, XOR, Iris), the quantum kernels matched the classical ones and did not exceed them, and the estimated kernel needs a number of shots that grows with the size of the Gram matrix. The advantage, where there is one, is in the data, as Huang and coworkers [Huang2021] say, and the model’s job is to not stand in its way.

The quantum approximate optimization algorithm. A variational eigensolver for a classical spin glass, whose ansatz is Trotterized adiabatic evolution and whose parameters are trained by the same loop as every later model (Chapter 3). At the depths we simulated it reaches the optimum of the smallest instances, an edge and a ring, but not of a six-vertex graph with a triangle; its landscape has the structure that warm starts exploit, and the per-term shift rule makes its gradients exact; what it does not do is beat the classical heuristics that solve these instances instantly, and the portfolio and clustering applications showed the model as a flexible optimizer rather than as a faster one.

The quantum neural network. A trigonometric polynomial in the encoded data whose frequencies are fixed by the encoding and whose coefficients are set by the trainable angles (Chapter 4). This single fact explains every experiment: the regression that an 18-parameter circuit fits at the noise floor and a 321-parameter network fits no better, the classifiers that match the kernel machines, the physics-informed circuits that solve a Poisson or a wave equation to machine precision when the solution lies in their function class and not otherwise. The barren plateaus of random circuits were reproduced with their exponential laws, and the natural gradient, imaginary-time evolution and stochastic reconfiguration were shown to be one equation.

The quantum Boltzmann machine. Statistical mechanics as a generative model (Chapter 5). On classical data the likelihood drove the transverse fields to zero and the model fell back on its classical limit, so that the training rule of the literature, the gradient of a Golden–Thompson bound, was good enough because the exact gradient wanted the same thing. On quantum data the quantum relative entropy was convex, Hamiltonians were recovered exactly from their thermal states, and the operator content of the model, non-commuting terms that no classical energy function can even represent, decided what could be learned.

The pattern is the one announced in Section Variational circuits reconsidered. The quantum models work as their theory says. On the classical data sets a textbook can hold, their gains over classical methods are gains of parameter economy or of structure rather than of accuracy, and in every case we could name the classical surrogate: the product of cosines, the single-layer product form, the classical Boltzmann machine. On quantum data (a Hamiltonian’s ground or thermal state, a kernel between quantum states, the operator content of a model Hamiltonian) there is no classical competitor to begin with, and the quantum model is not a replacement for a classical one but the only model there is.

The reassessment, with the evidence in hand#

Section Variational circuits reconsidered stated three connected difficulties of variational quantum machine learning and promised that the chapters would meet each in a concrete form. They did. Trainability: the gradient variances of Table 4.2 fall as \(2^{-n}\) and \(4^{-n}\) for the local and global costs, the kernel values of expressive feature maps concentrate exponentially (Section Perspectives), and the shot-noise study of Section Kernels from a finite number of shots shows what finite statistics do to a kernel matrix. Classical competition: every model that trained well in this book had a classical surrogate we could write down, and the published results on the link between provable absence of barren plateaus and classical simulability [Cerezo2025] and on classical surrogates of quantum convolutional networks [Bermejo2026] say that this is not an artifact of our small examples. Total cost: the kernels of Chapter 2 need \(\bigO(N^2)\) circuit runs of \(S\) shots each, the gradients of Chapter 4 need \(2P\) circuit evaluations per sample per step, and the Boltzmann machine of Chapter 5 needs a Gibbs state prepared anew for every gradient step; none of these costs appears in the classical competitor.

None of this is a theorem against variational learning. Avoiding barren plateaus does not universally imply classical simulability; the connection has been established case by case, for many commonly proposed architectures, and the surrogate sometimes requires a quantum data-acquisition stage that a classical computer cannot replace. The surrogate of a quantum convolutional network may still need quantum measurements of its input, and what it removes is the repeated quantum training loop. These results are serious challenges to particular designs. What they change is the burden of proof: a proposed quantum learning model must now say which quantum resource it uses and what that resource buys, and “it trains” and “it generalizes” are no longer answers.

A priority list for the next years#

Table 7.1 gives, for the next three to five years, the directions we find promising, in our order of priority. It is a judgment rather than a consensus, and each entry carries the qualification that we think decides it.

Approach

Why we find it promising

Main qualification

Learning quantum states, processes and dynamics

Quantum inputs avoid the classical data-loading bottleneck; coherent access and entangled measurements can give sample advantages

The advantages depend on the task and on the experimental access permitted

Quantum measurements followed by classical learning

Extract informative features once, then train classical models; often more economical than repeatedly optimizing a circuit

Some tasks need quantum memory or collective measurements that an ordinary measurement record cannot preserve

Structured variational models

Symmetry, locality, conservation laws and physical dynamics provide an inductive bias

Trainability and good predictive performance do not by themselves establish a quantum advantage

Quantum reservoir computing and fixed feature maps

Let quantum dynamics generate features and train mainly the classical readout; little optimization overhead

Measurement cost and strong classical reservoir baselines remain decisive

Fault-tolerant learning primitives

Coherent algorithms open possibilities beyond shallow noisy circuits

Practical value depends on logical hardware, data access and full resource estimates

Table 7.1: Directions in quantum machine learning for the near term, in our order of priority, with the reason for the ranking and the qualification that will decide each.

Learning quantum states, processes and dynamics. There is concrete evidence behind the first direction. The theory of learning from experiments [Huang2022experiments] shows an exponential separation in the number of experiments between an agent that can store and jointly measure copies of a quantum state and one that measures each copy and reasons classically, and the photonic experiment of Liu and coworkers [Liu2025photonic] realized, using entanglement, such a separation for the learning of bosonic displacement processes. This is evidence for learning quantum processes, not for a general quantum advantage on ordinary classification data, and the Hamiltonian learning of Section Learning quantum states is its simplest textbook instance: a convex problem whose data are expectation values of the state to be learned. The sample-complexity results of Anshu and coworkers [Anshu2021] for Gibbs states, and the provably efficient classical learning of ground-state properties from measurement data [Huang2022provably], set the frame in which such claims should be made.

Quantum measurements followed by classical learning. The second direction follows directly from the classical-surrogate results: the essential quantum contribution may be acquiring information, with the useful learning done classically. Classical shadows [Huang2020shadows] are the tool, a randomized measurement protocol from which many observables of a state can be predicted from few copies, and the surrogate of the quantum convolutional network [Bermejo2026] is the example: the measurements of the input are quantum, the training is not. The qualification is that some tasks need a quantum memory or collective measurements that no record of single-copy outcomes preserves, which is exactly the resource that the first direction uses.

Structured variational models. Variational circuits retain a credible role here. Circuits that respect the symmetries of a learning problem [Larocca2022,Meyer2023symmetry] encode its structure and improve generalization in the examples studied, and for many-body physics the natural structure is particle number, spin, spatial symmetry and the observables of interest, which is the content of the physically motivated ansätze of the companion volume [QCbook]. The qualification is the one of Section The reassessment, with the evidence in hand: a structured circuit that trains and predicts well has not thereby been shown to outperform a tensor network or a classical network with the same symmetries built in.

Quantum reservoir computing and fixed feature maps. The fourth direction removes the optimization loop. A quantum system with fixed dynamics is driven by the input, its measured observables are the features, and a linear readout is trained classically [Fujii2017]; the circuit-QED demonstration of Carles and coworkers [Carles2026], with a transmon–resonator system generating nonlinear features and no quantum gradient-training loop, is a recent proof of concept. The kernel methods of Chapter 2 are the same idea with the readout a support-vector machine. Whether a quantum reservoir beats a classical one at the same measurement budget is the decisive, and open, question.

Fault-tolerant learning primitives. The fifth direction is the one of the original speedup claims of Section Why quantum?, read with Aaronson’s fine print [Aaronson2015] and Tang’s dequantizations [Tang2019] in mind. Coherent algorithms (phase estimation, amplitude estimation, the linear-system algorithm and quantum principal-component analysis [Lloyd2014qpca]) open possibilities that shallow noisy circuits do not have, and their practical value depends on logical hardware, on how the data are accessed, and on full resource estimates of the kind the companion volume makes for its many-body algorithms.

For many-body physics and quantum sensing#

For the interests of the readers this book was written for, the priority order above has a sharper form. We would prioritize quantum process and Hamiltonian learning and adaptive experiment design, and compare structured variational models against measurement-based classical learning on the same physical problems. The central question should be: which quantum resource reduces the number of experiments, improves the estimation accuracy, or lowers the total computation at a fixed accuracy? A proposal that cannot name the resource and the quantity it improves is not yet a proposal. The models of this book that pass the test are the ones whose data are quantum: the kernel between quantum states, the Hamiltonian learned from its Gibbs state, the thermal state prepared variationally for a Hamiltonian of interest. The ones that do not, yet, are the ones that load classical data into a circuit, and they should be taught, as we have tried to teach them, alongside their trainability limits, their classical surrogates and the quantum-data alternatives. They remain useful tools; they are no longer a sufficient research strategy on their own.

Perspectives#

The perspectives of the companion volume and of this book are the same, seen from two sides, and we close with them.

Algorithms. On the near-term side: ansätze with the symmetries of the problem built in, error mitigation by zero-noise extrapolation and probabilistic error cancellation, remedies for barren plateaus by locality, layer-wise training and informed initialization, and benchmarks on tasks where the data are generated by quantum systems in the first place. On the fault-tolerant side: Hamiltonian simulation by Trotterization, qubitization and linear combinations of unitaries, randomized and Bayesian phase estimation with fewer qubits, quantum linear algebra read with its dequantizations, and quantum walks and search as subroutines of Monte Carlo.

Applications in the physical sciences. Electronic structure, nuclear structure with the Lipkin, pairing and shell-model Hamiltonians of the companion volume and of the many-body text [MBbook], strongly correlated systems beyond the reach of coupled-cluster theory, finite-temperature properties by imaginary-time evolution and thermal-state preparation, which may be the most natural application of the methods of Section Preparing Gibbs states on a quantum computer; lattice gauge theories and the Hubbard model, topological phases, and quantum sensing with Heisenberg-limited parameter estimation, where the learning-from-experiments results of Section A priority list for the next years apply most directly.

Quantum machine learning. Generative models beyond the restricted quantum Boltzmann machine, quantum generative adversarial and diffusion models; quantum reinforcement learning for quantum control and for adaptive experiment design, the subject reserved for Chapter 6; tensor networks and classical circuit simulation as both competitor and guide; and classical shadow tomography and cross-entropy benchmarking as the tools for certifying what a device has done.

Hardware. Superconducting, trapped-ion, photonic and neutral-atom platforms; surface and color codes and the first logical qubits; and the open question of how many logical qubits are needed for an advantage on a problem anyone cares about. Quantum advantage requires better algorithms and better hardware, in tandem, and the scale of fault-tolerant machines will decide which of the algorithms of this book are ever run on data too large to enumerate. The programs of the preceding chapters are written so that readers can run them on such a machine, or on its simulator, with the simulator of common/ replaced by a backend, and see for themselves whether the picture changes.
