!split
===== What is quantum computing? =====
!bblock
o It is computation (and more generally, information processing) based on the principles of quantum mechanics, rather than classical physics.
o Quantum mechanics is a description of nature at its most fundamental level.
!eblock
!bblock
o Formulated in the early 20th century to explain the behavior of subatomic particles.
o QM, and its specializations (quantum field theory, quantum chromodynamics, many-body physics etc.), have been spectacularly successful in explaining microscopic physical phenomena.
!eblock


!split
===== The exponentiality of QM =====
!bblock
o After nearly a century of study, the best (classical) methods for predicting the behavior of general quantum systems require exponential resources.
o The state of $N$ particles requires at least $2^{N}$ numbers to describe.
o For $N \sim 300$ (the number of particles in a single uranium atom),
  \[
    2^{300} \gg \approx 10^{82}\quad (\text{\# of atoms in the observable universe}).
  \]
o Nature seems to be doing extravagant amounts of computation *behind the scenes*!
!eblock


!split6 
===== The exponentiality of QM I =====
!bblock How do physicists actually do (quantum) physics?
o Answer 1: 100+ years of extremely clever approximations and shortcuts for calculations.
  * Examples: Bethe ansatz, Feynman diagrams, perturbation theory, mean field approximations, \dots
o Answer 2: computer simulations
  * Examples: Density functional theory, Quantum Markov Chain Monte Carlo
o Answer 3: studying systems that allow for Answers 1 and 2 above
  * Systems that are mostly classical
  * Systems with non-strongly interacting particles
  * Lucky that most things we’ve studied so far fall in this category!
!eblock
But simulating \emph{general} quantum physics is still  a hard task.

!split
===== The exponentiality of QM II ======
!bblock
In 1981, in his talk titled \emph{“Simulating Physics with Computers”}, Richard Feynman asked the following question:
_Can probabilistic computers simulate quantum mechanics?_.
!eblock
!bblock
His conclusion, after a lengthy exploration was that quantum mechanics [doesn’t] seem to be imitable by a local classical computer.
Instead we need a computer  built of quantum mechanical elements which obey quantum mechanical laws.
Feynman, along with David Deutsch, Paul Benioff and Yuri Manin, are credited with the idea of computing based on quantum mechanical principles.
!eblock


!split
===== The early days =====
!bblock
o Feynman’s motivation for quantum computers is the most obvious one: simulating quantum systems.
o To physicists in the '80s, this idea might have seemed obvious. To a computer scientist in the '80s, QCs would have seemed like a wild crackpot idea.
o Also in the '80s, David Deutsch contemplated other applications of quantum computers. He gave an example of a (classical) problem where the “parallel universes” of QM seem to speed up (by a constant factor).
o 1992: Dan Simon came up with an example of a problem that could be solved exponentially faster on a QC.
!eblock


!split
===== Shor’s breakthrough =====
!bblock
o 1993: Peter Shor realized that Simon’s idea could be extended to efficiently solve the following problem:
o \textbf{(Integer Factorization)} Given integer $N>0$, find integer $M>1$ such that $M$ divides $N$.
o The security of the RSA cryptosystem (the most widely deployed public-key encryption on the internet) crucially relies on the assumption that factoring integers is hard!
!eblock


!split
===== Crossroads =====
Since Shor’s algorithm, physicists and computer scientists have been faced with three options:
!bblock
o Quantum mechanics is wrong.
o There is a fast classical algorithm for factoring.
o Quantum computers are more powerful than classical computers.
!eblock

At least one of these must be true! Which do you think is most likely to be true?


!split11 ---
===== Crossroads}
!bblock
  o Option \#1: QM is wrong
  !bblock
    o Perhaps the most successful theory of nature to date.
    o In all its domains of applicability, we have never found experimental disagreement.
  !eblock
  o Option \#2: Polynomial-time classical factoring algorithm.
  !bblock
    o RSA has been out for nearly 50 years. There is enormous economic pressure to discover weaknesses, if it exists.
    o The “Gauss argument”: if the Prince of Mathematics couldn’t find it, then it probably doesn’t exist.
  !eblock
!eblock


!split12 ---
===== Crossroads}
!bblock
  o Option \#3: Quantum computers are more powerful than classical computers.
  o This would refute the Extended Church--Turing Thesis.
  o Church--Turing Thesis: A Turing machine can simulate any effective computation process.
  o Extended Church--Turing Thesis: A probabilistic Turing machine can efficiently simulate any effective computation process.
  o Option \#3 would violate the ECT (assuming factoring is hard for classical computers)!
!eblock

===== Emerging quantum computers}
!bblock
  o Until 2017 or so, most quantum computing devices had $\sim 10$ qubits.
  o Suddenly, there was a flood of resources devoted to engineering efforts, and now we are seeing programmable quantum computers with $50+$ qubits.
  o Major problem: devices are insanely noisy!
  o We are in the “NISQ” era:
  !bblock
    o Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum
    o Noisy devices with $\sim 100$ qubits.
  !eblock
  o Not capable of running Shor’s algorithm, but should still be capable of solving interesting, hard problems.
!eblock

===== Quantum supremacy}
!bblock
  o In Fall 2019, Google announced that they had achieved “Quantum Supremacy” using their 53-qubit Sycamore quantum processor.
  o \textbf{Quantum supremacy:} a moment when there is a convincing real-world demonstration of a quantum computing task that cannot feasibly be performed by a classical computer.
  o Sweet spot: 53 qubits just beyond the reach of Google’s compute clusters to simulate in a reasonable time. ($\sim 2^{53}$ ops)
!eblock

!split33 ---
===== Starting point}
\begin{center}
\large
Quantum information theory is a generalization of classical probability theory where probabilities can be negative, or even complex numbers.
\end{center}


!split34 ---
===== Starting point}
!bblock
  o Consider a physical system with $d$ distinguishable states, numbered $0,1,\dots,d-1$.
  o There is also an observer external to the system.
  o There are two things that can occur:
  !bblock
    o \textbf{Measurement:} the external observer can measure the state of $S$.
    o \textbf{Isolated evolution:} the system can change, without interacting with the external observer.
  !eblock
!eblock


!split35 ---
===== Classical physics}
!bblock
  o Initially, the observer assigns a state to the system $S$.
  o According to classical physics, we can model the state of $S$ as a probability distribution over states, represented as a column vector:
  \[
    \mathbf{p}=
    \begin{pmatrix}
      p_0\\ \vdots\\ p_{d-1}
    \end{pmatrix}\in \mathbb{R}^d,
    \qquad p_i\ge 0,\quad \sum_{i=0}^{d-1}p_i=1.
  \]
!eblock


!split36 ---
===== Classical physics}
!bblock
  o If the observer measures (i.e.\ “take a peek at”) the system $S$, then obtains a measurement outcome $i$ with probability $p_i$,
  o and then the state of the system gets updated to
  \[
    \mathbf{p}\mapsto \mathbf{p}' = \mathbf{e}_i
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
      0\\ \vdots\\ 1\\ \vdots\\ 0
    \end{pmatrix},
  \]
  where the $1$ is in the $i$th position.
  o If the observer measures again, then $S$ gets state $i$ with probability $1$ (nothing has changed).
!eblock


!split37 ---
===== Classical physics}
!bblock
  o If the system undergoes isolated evolution (i.e.\ “following the laws of physics”), then the state of the system gets updated via multiplication by a stochastic matrix $A$:
  \[
    \mathbf{p}\mapsto \mathbf{p}' = A\,\mathbf{p}.
  \]
  o A matrix is \emph{stochastic} if entries are nonnegative, and each column sums to $1$.
  o Stochastic matrices map probability vectors to probability vectors.
!eblock


!split38 ---
===== Quantum physics}
!bblock
  o Initially, the observer assigns a state to the system $S$.
  o According to quantum physics, we can model the state of $S$ as a complex unit vector in $\mathbb{C}^d$, represented as a column vector:
  \[
    \lvert \psi\rangle =
    \begin{pmatrix}
      \alpha_0\\ \vdots\\ \alpha_{d-1}
    \end{pmatrix},\qquad
    \sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\lvert \alpha_i\rvert^2 = 1.
  \]
  o The $d$ distinguishable states (also called “classical states”) are represented by the standard basis vectors
  \[
    \lvert 0\rangle =
    \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\ \vdots\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad
    \lvert 1\rangle =
    \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\ \vdots\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad \dots,\quad
    \lvert d-1\rangle =
    \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\ \vdots\\1\end{pmatrix}.
  \]
  o A general quantum state is a superposition of classical basis states:
  \[
    \lvert \psi\rangle = \alpha_0\lvert 0\rangle + \alpha_1\lvert 1\rangle + \cdots + \alpha_{d-1}\lvert d-1\rangle.
  \]
  o The $\alpha_i$’s are called \emph{amplitudes}.
!eblock


!split39 ---
===== Dirac notation}
!bblock
  o The notation $\lvert \psi\rangle$ is called \emph{Dirac notation}, used to represent quantum states.
  o Mathematically, a “ket vector” $\lvert \psi\rangle$ is a column vector.
  o The dual/Hermitian conjugate of column vectors (i.e.\ row vectors) are called “bra vectors”:
  \[
    \langle \psi \rvert = (\lvert \psi\rangle)^\dagger.
  \]
  o Example (qubit basis):
  \[
    \langle 0\rvert = (1,0),\qquad \langle 1\rvert = (0,1).
  \]
  o Inner product notation:
  \[
    \langle \phi \mid \psi\rangle \in \mathbb{C}.
  \]
  o Naming: “bra” + “ket” = “bracket”.
!eblock


!split40 ---
===== Quantum physics}
!bblock
  o If the observer measures the system $S$, then obtains a measurement outcome $i$ with probability
  \[
    \Pr[i] = \lvert \alpha_i\rvert^2,
  \]
  and then the state of the system gets updated (“collapsed”) from $\lvert \psi\rangle$ to the classical state $\lvert i\rangle$.
  o If the observer measures again, then it gets state $i$ with probability $1$.
  o This is called the \textbf{Born Rule}.
!eblock


!split41 ---
===== Quantum physics}
!bblock
  o If the system undergoes isolated evolution (i.e.\ “following the laws of physics”), then the state gets updated via multiplication by a unitary matrix $U$:
  \[
    \lvert \psi\rangle \mapsto \lvert \psi'\rangle = U\lvert \psi\rangle.
  \]
  o A complex matrix $U$ is \emph{unitary} if
  \[
    U^\dagger U = I \qquad (\text{equivalently } U^{-1}=U^\dagger).
  \]
  o $U^\dagger$ denotes the Hermitian conjugate: transpose then complex-conjugate every entry.
  o Unitary matrices map unit vectors to unit vectors.
!eblock


!split42 ---
===== Measuring a qubit}
!bblock
  o Example state:
  \[
    \lvert \psi\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\lvert 0\rangle + \sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}\lvert 1\rangle.
  \]
  o After measurement:
  !bblock
    o Outcome $0$ with probability $\frac{2}{3}$, post-measurement state $\lvert 0\rangle$.
    o Outcome $1$ with probability $\frac{1}{3}$, post-measurement state $\lvert 1\rangle$.
  !eblock
!eblock

\begin{frame}[t]{What is a quantum state describing?}
!bblock
  o A classical state is a probability distribution describing our uncertainty about the “true” underlying state of system S.
  o When the observer measures, the “true” state is revealed. However, intermediate measurements affect outcomes.
  o Quantum states are different: intermediate measurements do alter the system and cause different outcomes than if you didn’t measure.
  o Thus quantum states are not describing uncertainty of a system. There is no “true” underlying classical state.
  o But as external observers, we can only see ”classical shadows” of quantum states through measurement.
!eblock
